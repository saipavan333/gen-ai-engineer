Frontier & Responsible AI · Lesson 13.2

The modern frontier

The field moves fast enough that specifics go stale in months. This lesson isn’t a news roundup — it’s a durable map of the shifts that matter to a builder, and a way to stay current without drowning in hype.

School-kid picture

Choosing between a closed API model and an open-weight model is like choosing between eating at a restaurant and cooking at home. The restaurant has the best chef and no washing up, but you pay per plate, they change the menu when they like, and your order leaves the house. Cooking at home needs a kitchen and some skill, but you control the recipe and nobody else sees what you eat. The analogy breaks because home cooking is always cheaper per meal, while running your own model only gets cheaper at enough volume.

The shifts worth tracking

A working engineer doesn’t need to chase every release, but should understand the directions the field is moving, because they change design decisions.

Long context windows. Context windows have grown from a few thousand tokens to hundreds of thousands or millions. This is genuinely useful — you can feed whole documents or codebases — but it doesn’t make RAG obsolete, a claim you’ll hear and should push back on. Stuffing a million tokens is slow, expensive, and suffers “lost in the middle” (Track 11); retrieval is still how you keep context small, cheap, relevant, and current. The shift changes the balance (you can retrieve fewer, larger chunks), not the principle.

More capable agents. Tool use and multi-step reliability keep improving, making the agent patterns from Tracks 4 and 12 more practical — but the failure modes (compounding errors, loops, cost) and the mitigations (bounds, validation, evals) remain.

Small, efficient, and open models. Alongside the giant frontier models, small models keep getting more capable — many now run on a laptop or phone. Combined with strong open-weight models (whose parameters you can download and run yourself), this makes cheap, private, local AI increasingly viable — exactly the free/local stack this course uses.

Multimodal by default and reasoning as a dial. Text-only is becoming the exception; models increasingly see and hear (Track 10), and inference-time reasoning (Lesson 13.1) is now a lever you choose. The frontier is less “one bigger model” and more “a toolbox of capabilities you compose.”

Open vs closed: a real design choice

One decision this surfaces is open-weight vs closed (API-only) models. Closed models (accessed over an API) often have the highest peak capability and require no infrastructure, but you send data to a third party, pay per token, and can’t control versioning. Open-weight models you run yourself give you control, privacy (data never leaves your environment), predictable cost at scale, and no vendor lock-in — at the price of running the infrastructure and, often, somewhat lower peak capability.

The right answer is use-case-dependent: a privacy-sensitive internal tool may demand open/local; a low-volume feature needing top capability may favor a closed API. Being able to reason about that trade-off is a senior signal.

At real scale: Open-weight or API for an internal HR assistant at 30,000 employees

Assume 30,000 employees, each asking 5 questions a working day, with about 6,000 input tokens (retrieved policy text plus history) and 300 output tokens per question. Hardware costs below are placeholders you must replace with your own quotes.

  1. Volume. 30,000 x 5 = 150,000 questions a day. Input = 150,000 x 6,000 = 900M tokens. Output = 150,000 x 300 = 45M tokens.
  2. API cost. On Claude Sonnet 5.5 at $2 in / $10 out per million (Anthropic models overview): 900 x $2 = $1,800 plus 45 x $10 = $450, about $2,250/day, roughly $49,500 over 22 working days. Prompt caching on the shared instruction prefix and batch processing for non-urgent jobs would lower this.
  3. Self-hosted cost. Suppose your infra team quotes $X per GPU-month and estimates the open model needs N GPUs to hit your latency at peak. Self-hosting wins when N x $X plus engineer time is below the API bill. Get both numbers from a load test, not a blog post.
  4. Quality. Run the same 300-question HR eval on both. If the open model scores 90% and the API 94%, decide whether 4 points matters for HR answers. Often it does for policy, less for summaries.
  5. Privacy and control. HR questions contain personal data. Self-hosting keeps it inside your network; an API needs a data processing agreement and a check of retention terms.
  6. Long context check. A 1M-token window would let you paste the whole handbook. At 900M input tokens a day that is the expensive path; retrieval keeps each request near 6,000 tokens.
Key points
  • Track directions, not headlines: long context, stronger agents, small/open models, multimodal-by-default, reasoning-as-a-dial.
  • Long context ≠ the end of RAG — retrieval still keeps context small, cheap, relevant, and fresh; it shifts the balance, not the principle.
  • Small & open-weight models make cheap, private, local AI increasingly viable (this course’s stack).
  • Open vs closed is a real trade-off: control/privacy/cost vs peak capability and zero infra.
  • The frontier is a toolbox of capabilities to compose, not a single ever-bigger model.
Practice

A colleague says: “Context windows are huge now — let’s delete our RAG pipeline and just paste all the company docs into every prompt.” Give a measured response: when might large context help, and why keep retrieval?

▸ Show solution & reasoning

Where large context helps: it genuinely simplifies cases where the relevant material is modest and bounded — reasoning over a single long document, a whole small codebase, or a long conversation — where retrieval would add complexity for little gain. So the balance shifts: you can often retrieve fewer, larger chunks and lean on the model to find the needle.

Why keep retrieval: pasting all company docs into every prompt is slow (latency scales with tokens), expensive (you pay for every token, every call — potentially enormous at volume), and less accurate (irrelevant content distracts, and facts buried mid-context get missed — “lost in the middle”). It also can’t handle data that changes: retrieval re-indexes; a giant static prompt goes stale.

And most corpora simply don’t fit even huge windows. So the measured take: use large context to simplify where the content is small and self-contained, but keep retrieval as the scalable way to give the model only the relevant, current slice — cheaper, faster, fresher, and more accurate. It’s not either/or; it’s matching the tool to the size and freshness of the data. That nuanced answer — neither “RAG is dead” hype nor “never use long context” dogma — is exactly what marks an engineer who reasons from principles.

You keep hearing “huge context windows make RAG obsolete.” What’s the accurate view?

  • A) Long context shifts the balance but doesn’t replace retrieval — RAG still keeps context small, cheap, relevant, and up-to-date, and most corpora don’t fit anyway.
    Correct. Bigger windows help with bounded, self-contained content, but stuffing everything is slow, expensive, distraction-prone (“lost in the middle”), and can’t handle changing or oversized data. Retrieval remains the scalable way to supply just the relevant, current slice.
  • B) Correct — you should always paste all data in and never retrieve.
    That ignores cost, latency, distraction, freshness, and the fact that real corpora exceed even large windows. “Always paste everything” is the hype view the question is testing against.
  • C) Long context is useless; always retrieve one tiny chunk.
    Also too absolute. Large windows genuinely simplify some cases (one long document, a small codebase). The point is balance, not dogma in either direction.
  • D) Context window size has no effect on cost or latency.
    It very much does — more tokens means more money and more time per call. That cost is a core reason retrieval stays valuable at scale.

How do engineers stay current without drowning in every new model?

  • Invest in durable fundamentals; treat churning specifics (which model, prices) as lookups.
    Correct. The two-layer model keeps you sharp: deep fundamentals, look up the details when needed.
  • Read every paper the day it drops.
    That's the anxiety trap; fundamentals are what last.
  • Adopt every new model immediately.
    Evaluate on your use case first; don't chase headlines.
  • Ignore the field entirely.
    You track the top layer lightly, not ignore it.

A vendor announces a new model that tops a public leaderboard. What should you do before switching your production feature to it?

  • A) Switch immediately, since leaderboard rank means it is better at everything.
    Leaderboards cover specific tasks and can be gamed or saturated. They do not measure your use case.
  • B) Switch only if it has a bigger context window.
    Context size is one factor; quality, cost and latency on your task matter more.
  • C) Wait a year until the model is stable.
    Waiting by default means missing real gains. A quick eval answers the question in days.
  • D) Run it on your own golden set and compare quality, cost per request and latency with the current model before deciding.
    Right. Public benchmarks measure someone else's tasks. Your eval on your data is the only result that tells you whether it is better for you.
Deep dive: how to stay current without burning out

The anxiety of a fast field is real: it can feel like you must read every paper and try every model or fall behind. You don’t, and the engineers who last don’t either. The trick is a two-layer mental model. The bottom layer is fundamentals — tokens, embeddings, attention, sampling, RAG, agents, evaluation, safety, cost — and these barely change; a new model still predicts next tokens, still hallucinates, still needs grounding and evals.

Everything in this course is that layer, deliberately, because it’s durable. The top layer is specifics — which model is best, current prices, exact context sizes, new APIs — and this churns constantly. You keep the bottom layer deep and treat the top layer as lookups: when you need a specific, you check it, because you know it will have changed.

Concretely, this means when a shiny new model or technique appears, you don’t panic — you ask principled questions you already know how to answer: what capability does this add (more training, more test-time compute, a new modality, better tools)? what does it cost in money and latency? how would I evaluate whether it’s actually better on my use case (run it against my golden set)? does it change my architecture or just swap a component? That evaluation-first reflex — “I’ll measure it on my data” rather than “I’ll adopt it because it’s new” — is what keeps you current and grounded.

It’s also, not coincidentally, what makes you trustworthy to a team: the person who can calmly assess the frontier against real requirements is far more valuable than the one who chases every headline. Stay curious about the top layer, but invest in the bottom one — it’s where your durable value lives.

Exercises: warm-up, transfer, stretch

Warm-up. List three questions you would ask about any new model release before trying it on your product.

▸ Solution

(1) What capability does it add for my task: better reasoning, longer context, a new modality, cheaper price? (2) What does it cost per request and how fast is it at my prompt sizes? (3) How does it score on my golden set compared with what I run today? Optional fourth: does it change data handling or deployment, for example open weights I can host?

Transfer. Your company forbids sending customer data to third parties. Sketch an architecture that still uses a strong model for some tasks.

▸ Solution

Run an open-weight model inside your network for everything touching customer data: retrieval, summaries, classification. For tasks that need a stronger model, send only data that is not customer data (public docs, synthetic examples) or redact personal fields first and re-insert them locally after. Log what leaves the network. Evaluate whether the local model meets the bar on its own; often a retrieval upgrade closes more of the gap than a bigger model would.

Stretch. Argue both sides: 'small models will make most enterprise API usage unnecessary'. Then state what evidence would settle it for your company.

▸ Solution

For: many enterprise tasks are classification, extraction and grounded Q&A, where small open models with good retrieval and fine-tuning reach the bar, at low cost and with full data control. Against: hard reasoning, coding and long-context tasks still favour frontier models; self-hosting needs operations skill; and API prices keep falling. Evidence: per-task evals comparing a tuned small model with an API model, with cost per 1,000 requests including hosting and staff time, and latency at peak. The answer is likely different for each task.

Interview questions

Junior What is an open-weight model and why would a company use one?

▸ Model answer

An open-weight model is one whose trained parameters you can download and run on your own hardware, for example the Qwen2.5 family used in this course. Companies use them for privacy (data never leaves their environment), cost control at high volume, freedom to fine-tune, and no lock-in to a vendor's versioning. The trade-offs are running the infrastructure yourself and, often, lower peak capability than the best closed models.

Mid A colleague says 1M-token windows make RAG obsolete. How do you respond?

▸ Model answer

Large windows help with bounded material, like one long contract or a small codebase. But every token costs money and latency on every call, models can miss facts in the middle of long inputs, most corpora are far bigger than 1M tokens, and data changes. Prompt caching helps with a fixed prefix but not with a changing corpus. So long context shifts the balance, letting you retrieve fewer and bigger chunks, but retrieval stays the scalable default. I would test both on our questions.

Senior How do you keep a GenAI platform current without constant rewrites?

▸ Model answer

Put a thin abstraction between features and models, so a model is a configuration choice with its own prompt variants. Keep a golden set per feature and an automated eval harness, so a new model can be scored in hours. Track cost and latency per feature. Pin model versions and plan migrations, since providers retire models and change APIs. Review the landscape on a schedule rather than reacting to every launch. Decisions then come from measurements on our data.
